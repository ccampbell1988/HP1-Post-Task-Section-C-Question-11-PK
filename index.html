Question 11: Penalty Kill

Simple, Fast, Heavy - a four-player kill that blocks, clears and pressures on the trigger

Identity Personnel Faceoffs PP Systems PK Counters Forecheck Evaluation Drill Sources

Penalty Kill Identity: Simple, Fast, Heavy

Our team wins because it works harder than the opponent, and the penalty kill is where that identity is easiest to see. A gritty team should be dangerous shorthanded: four players connected in a tight shape, sticks in lanes, bodies in front of shots, and pressure that arrives together on a trigger. The structure is simple enough that any of our eight penalty killers can step in, read the same cues, and make the same calls without hesitation.

Our Penalty Kill Promise

We will win the draw or win the race to the box, protect the middle of the ice, block what gets through, and clear every puck we touch. We pressure only when the puck tells us to, and when we go, we go together.

Win it. Block it. Clear it. Pressure on the trigger. Four connected.

Four Non-Negotiables

  • No pass through the middle of our box: the bumper and the seam belong to us.
  • No puck held in our zone: one touch to clear (GLASS, RIM, or the length of the ice).
  • No pressure without a trigger, and no forward pressures alone without the shape shifting behind him.
  • No shot blocked with a hand or a turned back: square up, stick on the ice, finish the block.

1. Protect the Middle

Our shape is built to deny the bumper, the seam and the net front. We give up outside shots with no traffic.

2. Sticks in Lanes

Every killer's stick is on the ice in a passing or shooting lane. Our sticks kill more passes than our bodies.

3. Heavy at the Net

Our D win inside position on the net-front player, take away the goaltender's screen and clear rebounds.

4. Pressure on the Trigger

Bobble, back turned, bad-angle pass or a slow retriever: one player goes, the shape shifts, everyone moves together.

5. Clear and Change

Every clear is 200 feet and 10-15 seconds off the clock. Short shifts keep our legs fresh and our pressure real.

Personnel: Skills, Abilities and How I Use Players

Penalty killing is a role players earn. I choose killers for their habits (stick position, compete, reading the play, willingness to block shots) before their offensive skill. Each player knows his job in each formation, and I build two four-player units, a five-on-three unit, and identified faceoff specialists so the bench can manage every situation without improvising.

PK1 Unit

Use: the start of every penalty and the first 40 seconds.

  • Best faceoff forward and best pressure forward.
  • Best net-front D pair.
  • Kills against the opponent's top power play.
  • Plays the most dangerous situations (late in close games).

PK2 Unit

Use: the middle 40-60 seconds and the second half of the kill.

  • Two hard-working forwards who block shots.
  • Second D pair, same structure and calls.
  • Faces the opponent's second power play unit.
  • Gives development minutes to younger killers.

Deployment Rules

Rule: change on the clear, not on the clock.

  • Forwards 30-40 seconds; D 40-45 seconds.
  • Change forwards first on a long clear, then the D.
  • Never change all four at once during play.
  • A player who took the penalty does not kill it.

Faceoff Specialists

Rule: our best faceoff player takes every PK draw.

  • Left-shot centre on the left circle (forehand win back).
  • Right-shot centre on the right circle.
  • The specialist may double-shift for the draw and change on the clear.
  • Faceoff win % is tracked per player, per circle.

Developing Penalty Killers

Every forward and defenceman on the roster practises the PK in our weekly special-teams block, because penalties will take regular killers out of the lineup. I carry six forwards and four D as identified killers, give the second unit real minutes against the opponent's second power play, and promote players on the evidence: blocks, clears, stick-in-lane habits and goals against while on the ice. Our PK is a reward for work ethic, which reinforces the team identity.

Defensive Zone Faceoffs: Two Drills

Every penalty kill in our zone starts with a faceoff, and a win and clear can kill 15 seconds before the power play touches the puck. These two drills give the unit one plan for a win and one plan for a loss, so no player is ever deciding what to do after the drop.

All twelve diagrams are CoachThem drill diagrams. Drill 2, Diagram 3 and Drill 12 are original CoachThem drill-bank images, which have their own colour legend (explained in each drill's setup); the other nine are CoachThem diagrams of our own systems. In those nine, our PK players are black (F1, F2, D1, D2), the power play is shown as red X players and G is our goaltender; solid lines are skating routes, wavy lines are skating with the puck, dashed lines are backward skating, dotted lines are passes or clears, a dotted line ending in a triple arrow is a shot and the black bar marks the faceoff. In the zone diagrams our net is at the top of the drawing, and each player's job is listed in the card text. Diagrams can be mirrored for the right circle.

1
Defensive Zone Faceoff - Draw Won

Faceoff Win to Clear - GLASS or RIM

Objective: Turn every defensive-zone faceoff win into a clear within one touch, so the power play has to go 200 feet to set up again.

FaceoffsClearsPuck Control
Setup

Four PK players (F1, F2, D1, D2) take a defensive-zone faceoff in the left circle against a five-player power play and a goaltender. F1 takes the draw, F2 lines up on the wall-side hash, D1 sets behind the dot toward the boards, and D2 takes the inside hash. A coach holds extra pucks at the blue line. Run from both circles; rotate faceoff takers every three draws.

Detailed Drill Instructions
  1. Pre-draw call: Before the puck drops, F1 looks back and confirms the call with D1: "GLASS." D1 sets the feet slightly open to the boards so the puck arrives on the forehand. F2 and D2 confirm their tie-up assignments: F2 takes the wall winger's stick, D2 takes the inside winger's body.
  2. Win back to protected ice: F1 draws the puck back between the dot and the boards to D1's forehand. If the draw is clean, F1 immediately ties up the opposing centre's stick for one full second so the centre cannot jump the clear, then releases into the middle lane.
  3. One touch: GLASS: D1 does not stop the puck. The first touch goes off the glass on the strong side and out over the blue line. If the opposing point is holding at the line, the puck must be high enough to clear his stick. We are shorthanded, so icing is legal: an iced clear is still a clear.
  4. Read the pinch: RIM: If the strong-side point pinches down the wall before the draw, D1 or F1 calls "RIM" and D1 fires the puck hard around the end boards behind the net, up the weak-side wall. D2 drops to the net front while the puck travels, in case it is held in on the far side.
  5. Chase and change: F2 lifts the wall winger's stick, then sprints up the wall behind the clear to pressure the retriever and slow the regroup. F1 follows through the middle. On a clean clear, the coach calls "CHANGE" and a new unit of four replaces them; if the puck is held in, the same four snap into the box and play the possession live for 20 seconds.
  6. Live finish: After the clear, the coach may spot a second puck to the power play at the blue line. The PK must get set in its box before the first pass. A goal ends the repetition; a clear or a frozen puck earns the point.
Progression, scoring and rotation: Start 4-on-0 so D1 can repeat the one-touch glass clear 6-8 times from each side. Progress to 4-on-2 with a live opposing centre and point, then 4-on-5 full faceoff with the power play allowed to win and attack. PK earns 1 point for a clear within three seconds of the drop and 1 point for a successful change; the power play earns 2 points for a goal. Track the faceoff win percentage of each centre in the drill. Suggested block: 8 minutes.
3 Key Teaching Points
  1. Win to D1's forehand and tie up the centre's stick; a clean win is wasted if he jumps the clear.
  2. D1 one-touches the puck high off the glass or hard around the rim; no stickhandling below our goal line.
  3. Everyone wins their tie-up first, then F2 chases the clear and the unit changes on the clear.

Defensive Zone Faceoff - Draw Lost

Penalty Kill - Faceoff Loss

Objective: When we lose a defensive-zone draw, win every tie-up, take away the receiving defenceman's options and SET the box before the power play can make a play.

FaceoffsPressureCommunication
Setup

Four PK players line up for a defensive-zone faceoff in the left circle against a five-player power play. On the CoachThem diagram our PK is in black (two F, D1 and D2) and the power play is in red (three red F on the draw and two red D at the points). The black F on the dot (our centre F) takes the draw. D1 lines up on the wall-side hash only if the power play puts a red F on the wall; otherwise D1 starts closer to the front of the net (the diagram shows both D1 spots). D2 lines up on the inside hash, and the second black F (our wing F) sets up beside D2 on the inside. The red F on the dot wins the puck back to the red D on the strong-side point. Run from both circles.

Detailed Drill Instructions
  1. Read the alignment before the drop: D1 checks where the power play has lined up. If there is a red F on the wall, D1 takes the wall-side hash; if not, D1 moves toward the net front. Every player says the loss job out loud before the puck drops.
  2. The centre F ties up the red centre: On the loss, our centre F ties up the stick and body of the red F on the dot so he cannot get through to the slot or the net. The centre F holds the tie-up only until the puck has left the circle.
  3. D1 and D2 win their tie-ups: D1 ties up the red F on the wall and jumps on any loose puck to help the centre F; a loose puck is cleared with one touch (GLASS or RIM). D2 ties up the inside red F and holds the net front: D2 does not leave the front of the net.
  4. The wing F jumps on the red D: The wing F releases immediately from beside D2 and pressures the red D who received the draw. The angle takes away the walk to the middle and the D-to-D pass, so the red D has to make a hurried play to the wall or the corner.
  5. The centre F fills the middle and calls SET: As the wing F pressures across, the centre F releases off the red centre and fills the middle of the ice. The two black Fs talk the whole way ("I've got the D" / "Middle"), and once both are in position the centre F calls "SET." The four players are now in the box with sticks in the lanes.
  6. Kill the possession: Play is live for 20 seconds or until the PK clears or freezes the puck. If the PK recovers the puck, it is cleared with one touch, and the unit changes on the clear.
Progression, scoring and rotation: Walk through the loss at half speed with the coach dropping the puck straight back to the defenceman. Progress to a live draw where the power-play centre tries to win cleanly, then let the power play choose whether to win to the red D at the point or to the red F on the wall. PK earns 1 point for stopping the D-to-D pass or the walk to the middle, 1 point for a set box before the second pass, and 2 points for a clear; the power play earns 2 points for a goal. Suggested block: 8 minutes, both circles.
3 Key Teaching Points
  1. Win your tie-up first: the centre F stops the red centre from getting through, D1 jumps on loose pucks and D2 holds the net front.
  2. The wing F jumps right away on the red D who receives the draw, with no walk to the middle and no D-to-D pass.
  3. The centre F fills the middle as the wing F pressures across; the two Fs talk so the box is SET before the next pass.

Opponent Power Play Systems: Two 5-on-4 and Two 5-on-3

Our killers must recognise the formation in the first two passes. I teach these four power plays to the PK using our own power-play units in practice, and I tag them in pre-scouting video so players know what to expect. Each diagram shows the power play in our defensive zone with the puck on its usual entry side.

Opponent Power Play - 5-on-4

Power Play - 1-3-1 - Options 3 & 4

Objective: Show our penalty killers what a well-run opposing 1-3-1 looks like (full control at the top, a double-screen point shot and a post-up in the slot) and what our Diamond must take away.

5-on-4ScoutingThreat Recognition
System Overview

This CoachThem power-play drill is how a good 1-3-1 attacks us, so I use it with our second power-play unit to give the PK a realistic look. On the diagram, the black F and D tokens show where the five power-play players start, and the red tokens show where the same players set up for each option: a red RD at the top, a red LW and a second red RD on the walls, a red RW in the slot as the bumper and a red LW at the net (G is our goaltender; our killers are not drawn). The power play first gets the puck to the red RD at the top with full control, spreading our box by moving it D-to-D and back to the top. From there it runs two set plays. Option 3 (Double Screen), right end (it can be run from either side): the puck works low to high, and the red RD at the top shoots from the point through a double screen, with the red RW (bumper) and the red LW at the net staggered so the goaltender cannot see the puck. Option 4 (Post Up), left end: the red LW on the half wall moves the puck low and drives the net in a give-and-go; the red LW from the goal line carries the puck up the wall, waits for the net-driver to clear a lane, and passes to the red RW posted up in the slot.

Player Responsibilities
  1. Top player (red RD at the top): Receives the puck at the top in the middle with full control, moves it D-to-D with the other red RD to spread our forwards and gets it back to the top. On Option 3 he is the shooter: a low shot 4-6 inches off the ice that just has to hit the net.
  2. Half-wall players (red LW and the second red RD on the walls): On Option 3 they start the low-to-high movement. On Option 4 the red LW on the half wall moves the puck low and drives straight to the net, clearing a lane through our box.
  3. Bumper (red RW in the slot): On Option 3 he staggers with the red LW at the net as the second screen. On Option 4 he is the player who posts up in the slot to receive the shot pass.
  4. Low / net-front player (red LW at the net): On Option 3 he stands in the goaltender's eyes and staggers with the red RW. On Option 4 he comes out to the corner, carries the puck up the wall, waits for the net-driver to clear, then passes to the red RW in the slot.
  5. What our PK must take away: Our killers are not drawn on this diagram; our counter is the Diamond (Diagram 7). Our top forward takes away the top-middle so the red RD never gets full control there. Our bottom D boxes out the red LW at the net so the screens cannot stagger, and the diamond's sides get sticks in the point-shot lane. On the post-up, our D do not follow the net-driving red LW out of the slot: they stay on the red RW posted in the middle.
How I use it: Our PK practises against our second power-play unit running these two options at half speed first, then live 4-on-5 for 45-second shifts. Before each game I check whether the opponent's 1-3-1 uses a double-screen or post-up play, and the PK walks through the matching Diamond reads in the pre-game skate.
3 Key Teaching Points
  1. They outnumber us to get control at the top, so our top forward takes away the middle of the blue line before the red RD is set.
  2. They move the puck to the red RD at the point for a low shot through a double screen, so we get sticks in the lane and box out the red RW and red LW screens.
  3. They move the puck to the middle on the post-up, so our D stay home on the red RW in the slot and do not chase the net-driving red LW.

Opponent Power Play - 5-on-4

Umbrella Power Play (3 High, 2 Low)

Objective: Recognise the umbrella and its one-timer lanes so we take away shooters, not just space.

5-on-4ScoutingShot Lanes
System Overview

The umbrella puts three players across the top (a quarterback, QB, in the middle and two umbrella wings, W, at the tops of the circles) and two players low (a low player, LOW, near the goal line and a net-front screen, NET). It is a shooting power play: the puck moves across the top until a shooter gets a clean one-timer through traffic, with the NET player screening and the LOW player pulling our box down to open low-to-high plays. It is effective against a box that sags, because the three high players can out-number our two high forwards.

Player Responsibilities
  1. Quarterback (QB): Middle of the blue line. Moves the puck side to side, shoots through the screen, and resets when the lanes close.
  2. Umbrella wings (W): Set up at the tops of the circles, ideally on their one-timer side. They catch passes from the QB or from the LOW player and shoot immediately.
  3. Low player (LOW): Plays near the goal line on the strong side. Receives from the W, then returns the puck up (low-to-high) once our box has collapsed, or walks out from the goal line.
  4. Net front (NET): Stands in the goaltender's eyes for every point shot, tips, and fights for rebounds.
  5. Main patterns: W to QB to the far W for a one-timer; W down to LOW and back up for a shot; QB shot through the NET screen with a tip.
What it means for our PK: The umbrella wins with time and lanes at the top. We cannot let three shooters face two forwards with no pressure. Our counter is the Wedge Plus One (Diagram 8): one forward pressures on the trigger, three players protect the slot, and we block what gets through.
3 Key Teaching Points
  1. Count the high players: three high (QB and two W) means an umbrella, and the weak-side W is the one-timer threat.
  2. Shooters need time and a lane; our pressure takes away time and our sticks take away the lane.
  3. Low-to-high is their reset: our D cannot be pulled below the goal line.

Opponent Power Play - 5-on-3

2-1-2 Five-on-Three

Objective: Understand how a two-player advantage stretches our three killers and where the back-door threat comes from.

5-on-3ScoutingNet-Front Defence
System Overview

On a five-on-three the power play does not need to be fancy; it needs to move the puck quickly enough that our three killers cannot cover the shot lanes and the back door at the same time. The 2-1-2 places two points (PT) high, a bumper (BMP) in the middle of the slot, and two players low: one on the strong-side goal line (GL) and one at the weak-side back door (BD). The points shoot one-timers through the middle, and the low players look for a back-door tap-in when our triangle shifts to the puck.

Player Responsibilities
  1. Two points (PT): Spread to the tops of the circles or the blue line. They move the puck PT-to-PT for one-timers and shoot through the BMP's screen.
  2. Bumper (BMP): Sits in the middle of our triangle so one of our players has to choose between him and a shooter. He shoots, tips, or relays to the low players.
  3. Goal-line player (GL): Strong-side goal line. When he gets the puck, he looks for the pass across the crease to the BD or the BMP in the slot.
  4. Back-door player (BD): Weak-side post. He stays out of the goaltender's view and waits for the cross-crease pass.
  5. Main patterns: PT-to-PT one-timer; PT to BMP; PT down to GL, then across to BD.
What it means for our PK: With only three players, we protect the most dangerous ice and give up the outside. The back door and the bumper are the two goals we cannot allow. Our counter is the Inverted Triangle (Diagram 9), which rotates into a regular triangle when the puck goes low.
3 Key Teaching Points
  1. On a five-on-three, the most dangerous pass is across the crease.
  2. Two points mean two one-timer lanes; we block lanes with sticks and bodies, not by chasing.
  3. Identify the BMP immediately: he decides whether we play inverted or regular.

Opponent Power Play - 5-on-3

1-2-2 Five-on-Three

Objective: Recognise the 1-2-2 and its cross-seam one-timer so our triangle stays tight and our high forward owns the seam.

5-on-3ScoutingSeam Defence
System Overview

The 1-2-2 uses one quarterback (QB) high, two flanks (FL) at the tops of the circles and two low players: one on the goal line (GL) and one at the net front or back door (NET). Against three killers, the flanks are the shooters. The power play moves the puck FL to QB to FL, or straight across the seam for a one-timer, while the two low players pull our two defence away from the slot.

Player Responsibilities
  1. Quarterback (QB): Holds the top of the zone, resets the play and keeps our high forward honest by threatening the middle shot.
  2. Flanks (FL): Set up on the one-timer side at the tops of the circles. The cross-seam pass between them is the primary goal-scoring play.
  3. Goal-line player (GL): Pulls our strong-side D down and makes the low pass to the NET player at the back door or into the slot.
  4. Net front / back door (NET): Screens and tips, then slides to the back door when the puck is on the far side.
  5. Main patterns: FL to far FL (seam one-timer); FL to GL to NET at the back door; QB one-timer through traffic.
What it means for our PK: The 1-2-2 is built to beat a triangle that is spread too wide. We stay tight, keep our high forward in the middle on the seam, and rotate only on the low pass. Our counter is the Tight Triangle with Rotation (Diagram 10).
3 Key Teaching Points
  1. The seam pass is the threat: a stick in the middle takes it away.
  2. Two low players try to pull both of our D; only one D ever leaves the net.
  3. Outside shots through no traffic are acceptable on a five-on-three; passes across the slot are not.

Our PK System Against Each Power Play

Our base formation is the box. Against each of the four power plays, we adjust the shape to take away what that formation is built to do. The adjustments use the same players, the same calls and the same pressure triggers, so the system stays simple.

Versus the 1-3-1

Diamond. The bumper lives inside our shape. NO-GO is the base read, and we pressure the flank on a trigger with F1 squeezing the middle.

Versus the Umbrella

Wedge Plus One. One forward pressures the shooter with the puck; three players protect the slot. The forwards swap jobs when the puck goes to the quarterback.

Versus a Five-on-Three

Triangle. Inverted (two high) against two points, regular and tight (one high) against one quarterback. One rotation on the low pass; the back door is never open.

Our PK vs 1-3-1 (4-on-5)

Diamond with Bumper Denial - GO / NO-GO

Objective: Take away the middle of the 1-3-1, force the puck to the walls, and pressure only on a clear trigger.

DiamondPressure on TriggerShot Blocking
System Overview

Against the 1-3-1 we play a diamond (1-2-1). F1 is the top of the diamond between the quarterback (QB) and the bumper (BMP); F2 is the strong side of the diamond on the puck flank (FL); D1 is the weak side, responsible for the seam; D2 is the bottom of the diamond on the net-front player (NET). The BMP ends up inside our shape, where he cannot receive a pass. Our base read is NO-GO: hold the shape, sticks in lanes, and live with a shot from the FL on the half wall. When there is a trigger, we call GO: F2 pressures the FL and F1 SQUEEZES down on the BMP.

Player Responsibilities
  1. F1 (top): Stays between the QB and the BMP with the stick in the passing lane. Shades toward the puck side. On GO, F1 drops onto the BMP's stick so the FL's escape pass is gone.
  2. F2 (strong side): Stays between the puck-side FL and the net, about a stick-and-a-half away. GO triggers: bobbled puck, back turned to us, a bad-angle pass, or the FL receiving on the backhand. Pressure goes through the hands, not past the body.
  3. D1 (weak side): Owns the seam lane and the weak-side FL's one-timer. Stays on the inside shoulder of the weak-side FL; if the seam pass is made, D1 must already be in the shot lane, sliding to block on the stick side.
  4. D2 (bottom): Body on the NET player: inside position, stick on the ice in the low lane. Clears rebounds to the corners. Never leaves the net to chase.
  5. Rotation: If the puck goes to the far FL, the diamond rotates: D1 becomes the strong side, F2 becomes the weak side, F1 shifts across the top. If the puck goes low to the NET player, D2 pressures and the nearest side player drops to the front of the net.
  6. Clear: Any recovered puck is cleared with one touch: GLASS, RIM, or down the ice. We are shorthanded, so icing is our friend.
How I teach it: Walk-through 4-on-5 with the coach moving the puck by hand to each spot, players freezing to show their position. Progress to live puck movement with GO/NO-GO called by D2 (who sees the whole ice). Finish 4-on-5 live for 45-second shifts. I pre-scout the opponent's flanks for handedness; an off-hand FL changes where F2 and D1 shade.
3 Key Teaching Points
  1. The BMP never touches the puck: F1's stick and our shape take that pass away.
  2. NO-GO is our base: hold the diamond. GO only on a clear trigger, and F1 squeezes when F2 goes.
  3. Every recovered puck leaves the zone in one touch.

Our PK vs Umbrella (4-on-5)

Wedge Plus One - Pressure the Shooter

Objective: Take time away from the umbrella's shooters with one forward while three players protect the slot.

Wedge +1Pressure on TriggerShot Blocking
System Overview

Against the umbrella we start in a box and, on the trigger, move into a WEDGE PLUS ONE. The strong-side forward (F2) is the plus one: he attacks the umbrella wing (W) with the puck. The other three form a wedge that protects the slot: F1 slides into the high slot in the quarterback's (QB) shot lane, D1 sits between the low player (LOW) and the net, and D2 boxes out the net-front player (NET). The wedge gives up the outside and the LOW player, but it keeps three bodies between the puck and the net and takes away the one-timers. Because the umbrella has three shooters high, we can only pressure one at a time: the plus one goes on the trigger, and F1 rotates out to the weak-side W when the puck goes to the QB.

Player Responsibilities
  1. F2 (+1 pressure): On the trigger (pass to the W, bobble, or the W turning to the wall), attack the W on his stick side with the stick in the lane to the net. Take away the shot first, then the pass.
  2. F1 (wedge top): High slot, in the QB's lane. When the puck goes to the QB, F1 rotates out to the weak-side W and F2 recovers to the middle: the forwards swap jobs, they do not chase together.
  3. D1 (strong low): Stays between the LOW player and the net. Denies the low-to-high return and the walk-out. Will not chase below the goal line unless the puck is loose.
  4. D2 (net front): Inside position on the NET player. Must see the puck to block or tie up the stick on a point shot, and clears rebounds.
  5. Shot blocking: Shots from the top are blocked by the forwards in the lane: get square, stick on the ice, and use the shins and pads (not a hand). Low shots are the goaltender's; we clear the second puck.
  6. Clear and change: On a block, the nearest player gets the loose puck out and the unit changes if the shift is over 30 seconds.
How I teach it: Start with a box against a static umbrella and the coach calling "WEDGE" to rehearse the shape change. Progress to live 4-on-5 with the power play shooting only from the top (to build blocking), then remove the restriction. I use the Ice Hockey Systems Wedge Plus 1 sequences as a reference for the rotation.
3 Key Teaching Points
  1. One forward pressures; the other three protect the slot. Never two forwards on the puck.
  2. When the puck goes to the QB, the forwards swap jobs: F1 out, F2 back to the middle.
  3. Block shots square to the shooter, stick on the ice, and clear the second puck.

Our PK vs 2-1-2 (3-on-5)

Inverted Triangle - Rotate When the Puck Goes Low

Objective: Survive a five-on-three against two point shooters by blocking both shot lanes, squeezing the bumper, and flipping the triangle when the puck goes low.

TriangleRotationShot Blocking
System Overview

Five-on-three is played by our 3-on-5 unit (our best faceoff forward plus two defence). Against the 2-1-2, with the puck at the points, we play an INVERTED TRIANGLE (two high, one low). F1 takes the shot lane of the point (PT) with the puck, D1 takes the weak-side PT's one-timer lane, and D2 is the low player in front of the net. The bumper (BMP) is squeezed between the three. When the puck goes down to the goal line (GL), the triangle flips to a regular triangle (one high, two low): D1 drops to the back door (BD), F1 slides to the middle of the slot, and D2 takes the near post. We give up shots from the outside; we do not give up the back door.

Player Responsibilities
  1. F1 (strong high): In the puck-side PT's shot lane, stick on the ice. Does not chase above the tops of the circles. When the puck moves PT-to-PT, F1 slides to the middle to take the BMP.
  2. D1 (weak high): In the weak-side PT's one-timer lane. On a low pass, D1 drops to the BD before the puck arrives: this is the most important rotation on a five-on-three.
  3. D2 (low): Owns the crease and the low players (GL and BD). On a pass to the GL, D2 takes the near post and the cross-crease lane; D2 never leaves the front of the net.
  4. Goaltender: Plays the shooter on point shots; we tie up sticks on rebounds. Communicates "LOW" when the puck goes below the dots.
  5. Faceoff: Every five-on-three starts with a draw. Our best faceoff forward takes it, and a win is cleared the full length of the ice. A loss means SET into the inverted triangle immediately.
  6. Clear and time: Every clear is worth seconds; we clear down the ice, never try to make a play, and change D only on a stoppage.
How I teach it: Walk-through with the coach passing to each of the five spots, the unit calling "UP" (inverted) or "LOW" (regular) on every pass. Progress to live 3-on-5 for 30-second bursts with a running clock. Because five-on-threes are rare, we rehearse this for five minutes every week so the rotation stays automatic.
3 Key Teaching Points
  1. Two high in the shot lanes when the puck is up; flip to two low when the puck goes low.
  2. The back door is never open: D1's drop happens on the pass, not after the catch.
  3. Win the draw and clear the length of the ice; every second of a five-on-three counts.

Our PK vs 1-2-2 (3-on-5)

Tight Triangle with Low Rotation

Objective: Protect the middle against a 1-2-2 five-on-three: one high on the seam, two low at the net, and one rotation on the low pass.

TriangleRotationSeam Defence
System Overview

Against the 1-2-2 we play a regular TIGHT TRIANGLE: F1 high in the middle of the slot, shading toward the puck flank (FL) with the stick on the seam; D1 low strong side; D2 low weak side on the net-front player (NET). The triangle stays inside the dots so the FLs' only open shots come from the outside, with no seam pass available. When the puck goes to the goal line, we rotate: D1 pressures the goal-line player, GL (GO), F1 drops into the low slot to cover the space D1 left, and D2 holds the back door. When the puck goes back up, the triangle re-forms.

Player Responsibilities
  1. F1 (high): Middle of the slot, never higher than the tops of the circles. Stick on the seam so the cross-ice one-timer is blocked. Takes the quarterback's (QB) lane when the puck is up top.
  2. D1 (low strong): Between the GL player and the net. GO on the pass to the GL: force the puck back up the wall. On a pass back up, D1 returns to the low strong spot.
  3. D2 (low weak): Body on the NET player (net front / back door), stick in the cross-crease lane. D2 never leaves the net, even when the puck is on the far wall.
  4. Rotation rule: Only one player leaves the triangle at a time. If D1 goes, F1 drops; if F1 jumps to a FL on a bobble, D1 slides up to the slot.
  5. Clears: Any loose puck is cleared off the glass or down the ice; we do not carry it on a five-on-three.
  6. Communication: D2 runs the voice: "SEAM," "LOW," "BACK DOOR," "CLEAR."
How I teach it: Static walk-through, then live 3-on-5 for 30 seconds against the 1-2-2 with our second power-play unit. I alternate Diagram 9 and Diagram 10 in the same block so the unit learns to recognise the formation and call it on the ice: "TRIANGLE UP" against two points, "TRIANGLE" against one.
3 Key Teaching Points
  1. Tight and inside the dots: the seam is the goal we cannot give up.
  2. One player leaves the triangle at a time; the rotation is D1 out, F1 down, D2 holds.
  3. D2 owns the back door and the voice of the unit.

PK Forecheck: Two Drills

The best penalty kill is the one that keeps the power play out of our zone. Our forecheck is built to burn time and force dump-ins, not to chase. We play a tandem by default and pressure with two forwards only when the puck gives us a trigger.

Forecheck Decision Rule

No trigger = TANDEM. Trigger = GO + LOCK.

The first forward reads the retrieval. If the retriever is set and facing up ice, we stay stacked through the middle and steer. If the retriever's back is turned, the puck is bobbling or the dump is hard on the boards, F1 goes and F2 locks the middle passing lanes.

Forecheck Responsibilities

  • F1: steers the puck carrier to the wall from the inside, or pressures on GO.
  • F2: reads and takes the wall pass in the tandem; locks the middle passing lanes on GO.
  • D1 / D2: hold the red line, stand up at our blue line with a tight gap, seal the rim only when both forwards are committed.
  • All four: a dump-in is a win: retrieve, clear and change.

PK Forecheck - Passive Structure

TANDEM Forecheck - Steer, Read, Stand Up

Objective: Slow the power-play breakout, force it to one side, and deny a clean entry at our blue line so it has to dump the puck in.

ForecheckAnglingGap Control
Setup

Full ice. A five-player power play starts with a puck behind its own net (left end) and breaks out against our four-player PK. F1 starts at the top of their circles in the middle, F2 is stacked behind F1 near their blue line, and D1 and D2 start on either side of the red line. Our goaltender is in the far net. The coach at the bench rotates new power-play and PK units every repetition.

Detailed Drill Instructions
  1. Start in the TANDEM: On the whistle, the power-play defence starts the breakout from behind the net. F1 and F2 stay stacked in the middle: F1 at the top of the circles, F2 between their blue line and the red line. Neither forward goes below the dots: we are not trying to win the puck in their zone.
  2. F1 steers: F1 skates on an angle toward the puck carrier's inside shoulder, forcing him to the wall and away from a D-to-D or middle pass. F1 does not overskate the puck; the goal is to take away the middle and make the breakout predictable.
  3. F2 reads the side: As soon as the puck commits to one side, F2 shifts to that side and takes the wall pass or the winger on the wall. F1 and F2 now have the puck side outnumbered; the weak side is open, but the pass to it must go through the middle.
  4. D stand up: D1 and D2 hold the red line, then retreat with the play and stand up at our blue line with a tight gap. The puck-side D attacks the carrier's body as he reaches the line; the weak-side D protects the middle. No controlled entry is the goal.
  5. Force the dump and retrieve: If the power play dumps it in, the nearest D retrieves with a shoulder check and clears off the glass or the length of the ice; the partner protects the net front. If the carrier gets in with control, the PK immediately sets its box.
  6. Continuous version: After a clear, the coach passes a new puck to the power-play D behind the net and the same units go again. Three repetitions, then change both units.
Progression, scoring and rotation: Start 4-on-3 (power play without wingers) at three-quarter speed to rehearse the tandem shape. Progress to 4-on-5 live. Finish with a "shift clock": each PK unit stays out for 40 seconds and must win a clear to change. PK earns 1 point for a denied entry (dump-in or regroup forced), 2 points for a takeaway, and 1 point for a clear; power play earns 1 point for a controlled entry and 2 for a goal. Suggested block: 10 minutes.
3 Key Teaching Points
  1. F1 steers to the wall from the inside; F2 reads and takes the puck side. Neither forward chases below the dots.
  2. D stand up at our blue line with a tight gap; the puck-side D attacks the body, the weak-side D protects the middle.
  3. A dump-in is a win: retrieve, clear in one touch and change.

PK Forecheck - Pressure on Trigger

Red Wing 2v3 or 2v4 PK PP Game

Objective: Practise an aggressive two-forward PK forecheck against a three- or four-player power-play breakout in a fast, continuous game.

ForecheckPressureCompete
Setup

Full ice with one net (and a goaltender) moved onto the far blue line, facing the neutral zone, and a second goaltender in the far end net. Three or four power-play players (black X on the CoachThem diagram) start in the neutral zone. Two PK forecheckers (red F) start beside the net on the far blue line. The coach (C) has the pucks near the boards at the blue line. Extra groups wait at the bench.

Detailed Drill Instructions
  1. Start the play: The coach dumps a puck into the far zone. All of the power-play players skate back into their defensive zone to retrieve it, break it out and attack the net on the blue line. The two PK forwards leave from beside the blue-line net and forecheck.
  2. GO: attack the puck early: The first PK forward keeps his speed and attacks the puck early, arriving while the retriever is still turning. He angles from the inside, takes away the strong-side escape and finishes on the body. This is the aggressive forecheck I want: we do not wait for a trigger, we create one.
  3. LOCK: take away the middle: The second PK forward reads the first and takes away the passing lanes through the middle: the D-to-D pass, the centre swinging through the slot and the stretch pass. The power play is forced up the wall into more pressure or into a hurried rim.
  4. Play it out: If the power play breaks out, it attacks the blue-line net and the two forwards track back through the middle. If the PK wins the puck, it shoots on the far-end goaltender or puts the puck back deep. The repetition ends on a goal, a clean breakout and shot, or the whistle.
  5. Whistle: new group: On the coach's whistle, a new power-play group and a new pair of PK forwards jump on and the coach dumps the next puck. Shifts are short (20-30 seconds) so the forecheck stays at full speed.
  6. Freeze and teach: After every few repetitions the coach freezes a play to show where the middle lanes were and whether the second forward took them away. Start 2v3, then add a fourth power-play player (2v4) so the forecheckers have to read more options.
Progression, scoring and rotation: Run 2v3 until both forwards are connected, then 2v4. PK earns 2 points for a takeaway, 1 point for a forced rim, icing or turnover, and 2 points for a goal off a takeaway. The power play earns 1 point for a controlled breakout over its blue line and 2 points for a goal on the blue-line net. Rotate all forwards and defence through the power-play group so everyone feels the pressure. Suggested block: 8-10 minutes.
3 Key Teaching Points
  1. Keep your speed and attack the puck early (GO); the first forechecker arrives before the retriever can get set.
  2. The second PK forward takes away the passing lanes through the middle of the ice (LOCK).
  3. Work as a pair on short, all-out shifts; when the puck escapes, both forwards track back through the middle until the whistle.

How I Use Statistical Data to Evaluate the PK

Penalty-kill percentage tells me the result; it does not tell me why. A short sample of power plays can make a good kill look bad or a bad kill look good, so I track the process statistics that drive goals against and review them with video every week. The staff collects them live from the bench and confirms them from game video.

Live Bench Tracking

An assistant coach tracks clears, entries denied, faceoff wins and blocks on a one-page PK sheet during the game, so I can adjust units between periods.

Video Tagging

After the game, every PK is tagged: formation faced, our shape, triggers (GO or NO-GO correct), chances against by location, blocks, and clears.

Goals Against Review

Every power-play goal against is reviewed and assigned a cause: faceoff loss, failed clear, broken shape, missed rotation, no block, or a great play we accept.

Unit and Player Data

Chances against, blocks and clears are tracked per unit and per player, which decides who earns PK minutes and who needs more practice reps.

Opponent Scouting

Before each game, we tag the opponent's power play: formation, handedness of flanks, entry style and favourite play. The PK walks through the counter in the pre-game skate.

Trigger Decisions

Every GO and NO-GO decision is graded correct or incorrect on video. A correct decision that fails in execution is still praised; a wrong decision is corrected.

Practice Data

Drill scoring (clears within three seconds, set box before the first pass, denied entries) is recorded so practice and game numbers can be compared.

Discipline

Times shorthanded per game and penalty types are tracked. The best kill is the one we never need: we target fewer than four penalties a game.

Weekly Evaluation Cycle

Monday: staff review the data and the tagged clips. Tuesday: a 10-minute PK video meeting with three clips (one to celebrate, one to correct, one about the next opponent). Wednesday and Thursday: practice blocks target the weakest number, for example clears if our clear success dropped below 80%. Game day: pre-scout walk-through of the counter we will use.

Bottom line: our penalty kill is built to make a gritty team even harder to play against. We win the draw or win the race to the box, protect the middle, block what gets through, clear every puck and pressure only on the trigger. The data shows whether our habits are working, not only whether the puck went in.
